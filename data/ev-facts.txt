Electric vehicles (EVs) convert roughly 85% to 90% of electrical energy from the battery into mechanical motion at the wheels, compared to only 20% to 30% thermal efficiency in gasoline internal combustion engines.
Regenerative braking reverses the electric traction motor during deceleration so it acts as a generator, recovering up to 70% of kinetic energy that would otherwise be lost as heat in friction brakes.
In April 1899, a Belgian torpedo-shaped electric car named "La Jamais Contente" ("The Never Satisfied"), driven by Camille Jenatzy, became the first road vehicle in history to exceed 100 km/h (62 mph), reaching 105.88 km/h.
Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP) batteries contain no nickel or cobalt, tolerate repeated 100% state-of-charge cycles with minimal degradation, and offer superior thermal stability compared to Nickel Manganese Cobalt (NMC) cells.
Nickel Manganese Cobalt (NMC) and Nickel Cobalt Aluminum (NCA) lithium-ion chemistries provide higher gravimetric energy density (typically 240 to 290 Wh/kg at the cell level), making them popular for long-range flagship EVs.
Modern 800-volt EV architectures double the system voltage from traditional 400-volt packs, allowing twice the DC fast-charging power (up to 350 kW) at the same cable current while reducing copper wiring weight and resistive heat loss.
Under the SAE J3400 standard (North American Charging Standard, or NACS), a single compact connector uses shared power pins for both AC Level 2 charging and high-power DC fast charging up to 1,000 volts.
The charging curve of a lithium-ion EV battery tapers sharply above 80% state of charge (SoC) to prevent metallic lithium plating on the graphite anode, which is why highway DC fast-charging stops typically target 10% to 80% SoC.
Bidirectional charging enables Vehicle-to-Home (V2H) and Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) operation, allowing a typical 75 kWh EV battery pack to power an average household for 2 to 3 days during a blackout.
Unlike internal combustion engines that must build RPM to reach peak torque, an electric motor delivers 100% of its maximum torque instantaneously from 0 RPM, eliminating the need for a multi-speed transmission in most EVs.
Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSMs) use rare-earth neodymium rotor magnets to achieve over 95% peak efficiency, whereas AC Induction Motors use electromagnetic induction without permanent magnets and can freewheel with zero cogging drag.
Cold sub-freezing weather temporarily reduces EV driving range by 15% to 30% because lithium-ion electrolyte viscosity increases internal resistance and cabin heating draws power directly from the high-voltage battery.
Automotive heat pumps transfer ambient thermal energy from outside air and waste heat from the battery and inverter into the cabin, consuming 50% to 70% less electricity than resistive PTC cabin heaters in winter.
The EPA measures electric vehicle efficiency using MPGe (Miles Per Gallon equivalent), where 1 gallon of gasoline is defined as chemically equivalent to 33.7 kilowatt-hours (kWh) of electricity.
An EV that achieves 4.0 miles per kWh consumes just 8.4 kWh to travel 33.7 miles, earning an official rating of roughly 135 MPGe.
Battery preconditioning warms or cools the lithium-ion pack to its optimal temperature window (25°C to 35°C) before arriving at a DC fast charger, cutting winter 10%-to-80% charging times by up to 40%.
Solid-state EV batteries replace the flammable liquid organic electrolyte with a solid ceramic, sulfide, or polymer separator, enabling lithium-metal anodes that could reach 400 to 500 Wh/kg energy density.
Electric vehicles have no tailpipe emissions, and lifecycle studies show that even on an average coal-and-gas electrical grid, an EV offsets its higher battery manufacturing carbon footprint within 15,000 to 25,000 miles of driving.
High-voltage EV battery packs are isolated from the vehicle chassis and monitored continuously by a Battery Management System (BMS) that tracks individual cell voltages, temperatures, and isolation resistance every few milliseconds.
When an EV is unplugged or in a collision, pyrotechnic or electromagnetic high-voltage contactors inside the battery pack open within milliseconds to de-energize all external orange high-voltage cables.
Most electric vehicles use a 12-volt (or 16-volt / 48-volt) low-voltage auxiliary battery charged by a DC-to-DC converter from the main traction pack to power computers, lights, locks, and safety contactors.
One-pedal driving allows the driver to modulate both acceleration and up to 0.2g–0.3g of regenerative deceleration using only the accelerator pedal, bringing the vehicle to a complete stop without touching the friction brake pedal.
Because regenerative braking handles the vast majority of everyday slowing, friction brake pads and rotors on electric vehicles routinely last 100,000 to 150,000 miles—often twice as long as on conventional cars.
Aerodynamic drag scales with the square of velocity, meaning that at highway speeds above 65 mph (105 km/h), overcoming air resistance accounts for more than 60% of an electric vehicle's total energy consumption.
The Apollo Lunar Roving Vehicle (LRV) driven on the Moon during Apollo 15, 16, and 17 in 1971–1972 was a four-wheel-drive electric vehicle powered by two 36-volt silver-zinc batteries with a 0.25-horsepower motor at each wheel.
